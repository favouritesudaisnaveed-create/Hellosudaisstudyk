Topic 01

Cell structure and organisation

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of living organisms. Organisms may be unicellular (one cell) or multicellular (many cells).

Simplified animal cell — diagram is not to scale.

Main cell structures

  • Cell membrane: controls movement of substances into and out of the cell; it is selectively permeable.
  • Cytoplasm: where many chemical reactions take place, controlled by enzymes.
  • Nucleus: contains chromosomes made of DNA and controls cell activities.
  • Mitochondria: site of aerobic respiration and ATP production.
  • Ribosomes: site of protein synthesis.

Plant cells also have

  • Cell wall: made of cellulose; strengthens and supports the cell.
  • Chloroplasts: contain chlorophyll to absorb light for photosynthesis.
  • Permanent vacuole: contains cell sap and helps maintain turgor.
Remember: Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts (in photosynthetic cells) and a large permanent vacuole. Animal cells do not.

Levels of organisation

Cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. A tissue is a group of similar cells working together; an organ contains different tissues working together.

Topic 02

Biological molecules

Living organisms contain large molecules built from smaller units. The main groups studied are carbohydrates, proteins and lipids. Water is also essential to life.

CarbohydratesContain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Used as an energy source; starch is a plant storage carbohydrate and glycogen is an animal storage carbohydrate.
ProteinsMade from amino acids. Needed for growth and repair; enzymes are proteins.
LipidsMade from glycerol and fatty acids. Store energy, provide insulation and form cell membranes.
WaterA solvent and transport medium; it is involved in many chemical reactions and helps regulate temperature.

Food tests

  • Starch: add iodine solution. Positive result: orange-brown to blue-black.
  • Reducing sugar: add Benedict's solution and heat in a hot water bath. Positive result: green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate, depending on concentration.
  • Protein: use the Biuret test. Positive result: blue to lilac/purple.
  • Lipid: ethanol emulsion test. A cloudy white emulsion indicates lipid.
Exam tip: For food-test questions, state the reagent, any heating step, and the positive colour/result.
Topic 03

Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They speed up chemical reactions without being used up. Most enzymes are proteins and each enzyme has an active site with a specific shape.

The substrate fits the enzyme's active site; products are released after the reaction.

Factors affecting enzyme activity

  • Temperature: increasing temperature raises kinetic energy and collision frequency up to the optimum. Above the optimum, bonds maintaining the enzyme's shape break; the active site changes shape and the enzyme is denatured.
  • pH: each enzyme has an optimum pH. Very acidic or alkaline conditions can alter the active site and denature the enzyme.
  • Substrate concentration: rate increases as more substrate is available, until all active sites are occupied.
Key distinction: Denaturation is a change in the enzyme's structure and active-site shape. It is not simply the enzyme being used up.
Topic 04

Plant nutrition

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants make carbohydrates from carbon dioxide and water using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll.

Word equation:
carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
Light energy and chlorophyll are required.
Inputs and outputs of photosynthesis.

Limiting factors

  • Light intensity
  • Carbon dioxide concentration
  • Temperature

A limiting factor is the factor in shortest supply that prevents the rate from increasing further.

Mineral ions

  • Nitrate ions: needed to make amino acids and proteins. Deficiency can cause poor growth and yellowing of older leaves.
  • Magnesium ions: needed to make chlorophyll. Deficiency can cause yellowing of leaves.
Topic 05

Human nutrition

A balanced diet contains the right amounts of carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, vitamins, minerals, fibre and water for an individual's needs.

Digestive system

Simplified digestive-system route; organs are not to scale.

Route: mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → rectum → anus.

Digestive enzymes

  • Amylase: starch → maltose (a reducing sugar).
  • Protease: proteins → amino acids (via smaller peptides).
  • Lipase: lipids → fatty acids + glycerol.

Absorption in the small intestine

Villi and microvilli provide a large surface area. Their epithelium is thin, and each villus has a good blood supply. Lacteals absorb products of fat digestion.

Remember: Digestion breaks large, insoluble molecules into small, soluble molecules so they can be absorbed.
Topic 06

Transport in plants and humans

Transport in plants

XylemTransports water and mineral ions from roots towards leaves. Xylem vessels are dead, hollow tubes strengthened with lignin.
PhloemTransports sucrose and amino acids between sources and sinks. This movement is called translocation.

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves, mainly through stomata. Evaporation from leaf cells and diffusion of water vapour out of stomata help create a transpiration stream.

Human circulatory system

Humans have a double circulatory system: blood passes through the heart twice in one complete circuit — once through the lungs and once through the rest of the body.

Simplified double circulation. Arrows show the general route of blood flow.
  • Arteries: carry blood away from the heart; thick muscular and elastic walls.
  • Veins: carry blood towards the heart; thinner walls and valves.
  • Capillaries: very narrow vessels with walls one cell thick for exchange of substances.
Topic 07

Respiration

Respiration is the chemical process in cells that releases energy from food molecules. It is not the same as breathing.

Aerobic respirationUses oxygen and releases a large amount of energy.
Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
Anaerobic respiration in musclesOccurs without oxygen and releases less energy.
Glucose → lactic acid

Gas exchange in the lungs

Alveoli provide a large surface area, have thin walls, a moist lining and a rich blood supply. These features allow rapid diffusion of oxygen into blood and carbon dioxide out of blood.

Gas exchange by diffusion between an alveolus and surrounding capillary.
Topic 08

Excretion and homeostasis

Excretion is the removal of toxic materials, the waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements.

  • Kidneys: remove urea and regulate water and ion concentrations in the blood.
  • Lungs: remove carbon dioxide and water vapour.
  • Liver: forms urea from excess amino acids by deamination.

Urine formation

Blood is filtered in the kidney. Useful substances such as all glucose, some water and needed ions are selectively reabsorbed. Urea and excess water and ions remain in the filtrate and form urine.

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment, within narrow limits. Examples include regulation of blood glucose and body temperature.
Topic 09

Coordination and response

The nervous system coordinates rapid responses using electrical impulses. Hormones are chemical messengers carried in the blood and usually produce slower, longer-lasting effects.

Reflex arc

Simplified reflex arc sequence.

Pathway: stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → central nervous system → motor neurone → effector → response.

Eye and hormones

The eye detects light. The iris controls pupil size; the lens changes shape to focus light on the retina. Hormones are produced by endocrine glands and transported in the blood to target organs.

Topic 10

Reproduction

Reproduction produces new individuals. Asexual reproduction involves one parent and no fusion of gamete nuclei; sexual reproduction involves fusion of male and female gamete nuclei.

AsexualUsually genetically identical offspring (clones). It can be rapid and does not require a mate.
SexualOffspring show genetic variation because genetic material from two gametes combines.

Flower structure

  • Anther: produces pollen grains containing male nuclei.
  • Stigma: receives pollen.
  • Ovary: contains ovules; after fertilisation, ovules develop into seeds.
  • Petals: often attract pollinators.

Pollination and fertilisation

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from anther to stigma. Fertilisation is the fusion of the male nucleus with the female nucleus.

Human reproduction

In humans, fertilisation usually occurs in the oviduct. The zygote divides by mitosis and develops into an embryo. The placenta allows exchange of materials between maternal and fetal blood without the two blood supplies normally mixing directly.

Topic 11

Inheritance

Inheritance is the transmission of genetic information from parents to offspring. DNA is the genetic material; a gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein or functional product.

  • Chromosome: a long DNA molecule associated with proteins.
  • Alleles: different versions of the same gene.
  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has.
  • Phenotype: the observable characteristics, influenced by genotype and sometimes environment.
  • Dominant allele: expressed in the phenotype when one copy is present.
  • Recessive allele: expressed in the phenotype only when two copies are present (for a typical single-gene example).

Topic 12

Ecology and the environment

Ecology is the study of relationships between organisms and their environment.

  • Population: all organisms of one species in an area.
  • Community: all populations of different species in an area.
  • Ecosystem: a community and its physical environment.
  • Habitat: the place where an organism lives.

Food chains and energy

A food chain shows feeding relationships and the direction of energy transfer. Producers make their own organic nutrients, usually by photosynthesis. Consumers obtain energy by feeding on other organisms.

Example food chain.

Decomposition and nutrient cycles

Decomposers such as bacteria and fungi break down dead organisms and waste. This releases mineral ions back into the environment, where plants can absorb them.

Human impacts

Deforestation, pollution, habitat destruction and overuse of resources can reduce biodiversity. Conservation aims to protect species, habitats and ecosystems.
